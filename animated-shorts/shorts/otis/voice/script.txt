He climbed onto a platform, high above a crowd, and told a man to cut the rope.
You see, it's eighteen fifty-four, New York. Elevators exist, but nobody rides them.
Ropes snap. Platforms fall. They're for barrels, not people.
Then there's Elisha Otis. A mechanic in a bed factory, sick of watching heavy loads crash.
So he builds a lift with a weird iron spring on top.
And people laugh. "A safe elevator? Sure, buddy!"
Hardly anyone buys one.
So he hauls it to the biggest fair in America. The Crystal Palace.
He builds a tower, steps onto the platform, and rides it way up above the crowd.
Then he nods at his assistant. "Cut it."
Whack! The rope snaps. Women scream. Men grab their hats.
And the platform drops... a few inches. And stops.
Otis takes a bow. "All safe, gentlemen. All safe!"
Here's the trick. The rope was holding that spring flat.
The second the rope goes slack, the spring snaps open.
Iron teeth bite into notches along the rails.
No rope? No fall.
Three years later, the first passenger elevator opens in a New York store.
Then buildings start climbing. Ten floors. Twenty. A hundred.
Otis never saw them. He died just seven years after his stunt.
But every skyscraper on Earth started with "Cut the rope."
